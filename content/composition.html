Composition vs Inheritance

Throughout my time in studying for my major, inheritance is commonly taught as a way to introduce runtime polymorphism which, in layman's terms, is changing what a function does depending on who calls it. This typically follows a parent-child approach. For example, we have a parent class called Enemy, and each Enemy subclass (Zombie, Skeleton, etc.) has a few functions such as Attack(), TakeDamage(), and Move().

These seem straightforward enough and, at first, you might think this is the way to go. In fact, I've made this exact mistake in the past. However, let's take a step back and think about what we are doing when we use inheritance.

The Problem With Inheritance

What happens if we have a hundred different types of enemies, and some of them have exceptions to these rules? Maybe some enemies are invincible and can't take damage. Maybe some of them don't attack or even move, and are much more stationary with contact damage. How will we handle exceptions to the rules that we established? This is the pitfall of inheritance; you are signing a contract that forces every single child class to follow the same principles.

Composition as an Alternative

So what makes composition so special? Well, what if instead of having multiple classes and subclasses to handle variations of an enemy, we create separate objects such as MoveSystem, AttackSystem, and HPSystem. That way, we can pick and choose whether an enemy includes these systems, removing the contract that inheritance forces upon it, embracing potential changes in the future.

Conclusion

Does this mean inheritance is completely useless? No, of course not! Sometimes, you want that contract to be made for polymorphism to be used. Polymorphism is very useful when you want one line of code to work for several different types of objects, like one Drive() function call that works for all types of Car types. However, whenever you use inheritance, keep in mind the contract you are making. Like all things, use your knowledge with purpose.
